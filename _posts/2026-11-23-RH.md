*'Who can explain a 17-million-line proof of the Riemann hypothesis?'*

---

## A (really sketchy) timeline

| Year | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| **1859** | Riemann formulates its hypothesis [LINK](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/pub/HistMath/People/Riemann/Zeta/) |
| **2026** | Alpöge publishes [remark.pdf](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) with the link to a LEAN proof of the RH |



On Friday, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he had developed with Claude, Anthropic’s AI system. By Saturday, independent programs had checked the argument.   

The question itself fits on a single line. In 1859, Bernhard Riemann proposed that every nontrivial zero of his zeta function has real part ½. The zeros matter because their positions govern the error in our estimates of how many prime numbers lie below a given number. Riemann set out the connection in [six manuscript pages](https://www.claymath.org/collections/riemanns-1859-manuscript/). The Lean library that Alpöge released contains about *17 million lines of code*.

## Who did the mathematics?

In [his announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf), Alpöge gives enough detail to make the question of authorship interesting. He describes recognising how two ideas from a simpler problem might fit together and choosing the setting in which to try them. He credits Claude with a specific suggestion about an estimate involving a parameter *X*. The argument called for a chain of transformations known as *theta lifts*. How long should the chain be? Claude suggested roughly √*X* lifts. It is the sort of contribution a mathematician would want to discuss with a collaborator.

That same Friday, [Ravi Vakil announced](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/) a second approach. Alessandro Masullo had spent years writing [*The Music of the Primes*](https://sites.google.com/view/tmotp/home), a manuscript of roughly 900 pages proposing a proof of the generalised Riemann hypothesis. According to Vakil, Masullo had worked without AI. Brian Conrad had been reading the manuscript for months, and it was still under revision when it was made public.

Within hours, Vakil reported that readers using several AI models had identified problems. [Jenny Lorraine Nielsen’s critique](https://philarchive.org/rec/NIEIOM) singles out a concrete objection (just to quote one).  Whether Masullo’s strategy can be repaired remains open. Already the phrase “mathematics without AI” needs a qualification. It describes how Masullo wrote the manuscript. It tells us very little about how the manuscript was read.

## What Lean can settle

Claude helped devise the argument. Lean is a proof assistant whose rules specify when a conclusion follows from given premises. A mathematician working without generative AI can use Lean, just as a language model can write an ordinary paper with a gap in it. On 21 November, [Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative reported](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) that independent checkers had verified the proof. The reviewers also checked that its conclusion matched the formulation of the Riemann hypothesis already in Mathlib, the community’s mathematical library. 

For anyone who wants to use the theorem, this is an extraordinary outcome. A number theorist can inspect the statement, examine the assumptions and reproduce the verification. Confidence in the result can rest on evidence independent of Claude’s judgement, even for someone who has never used a language model.

## What would go on the blackboard?

Reading 17 million lines from beginning to end is beyond any realistic human review. Yet mathematicians routinely understand arguments that depend on more earlier work than one person could reconstruct. A good exposition tells us which earlier theorem to take for granted, where its hypotheses are needed, and which step we should stop and think about. In Alpöge’s example, a proof checker can certify that the chosen chain length gives the required estimate. A seminar audience will want to know what first made √*X* a promising length to try. 

The effort to recover those ideas is already public. There is a [Lean community discussion about simplifying the proof](https://leanprover.zulipchat.com/#narrow/channel/113488-general/topic/RH.3A.20cleaning.20up.20the.20proof/with/642814732). [Michael Rubinstein’s working guide](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~mrubinst/alpoge_claude_grh/), itself written with AI assistance, shows both the progress and the distance still to go. Its 22 November version isolates an inequality about a weighted sum over prime powers and reduces it to thirteen statements. The guide identifies what it takes from the Lean development and which arguments still need to be written out. A specialist can now pick one of those statements and ask what makes it true.

In [interviews collected by 3Blue1Brown](https://youtu.be/zqzk43bSdDI), mathematicians were asked what it would mean if AI solved the Riemann hypothesis. This weekend gave that thought experiment an unexpectedly precise form. We have a proof that independent programs can check, a human-written alternative whose difficulties readers can pinpoint, and mathematicians using AI to help read both. Imagine hearing, in that Monday seminar, why the square root appears and what should replace it if the problem is changed. That would be worth turning up for even if everyone in the room already accepted the theorem.

## So what?
I don't know what AI can do in 6 months. It will likely be able to explain better than us what are the main ingredients of the proof. What is clear is that the current proof uses a lot of results part of the Langalnds program and in particular a lot of p-adic Hodge theory (which is what I studied in the past 10 years). All of this was developed in the last 50 years, and  some results (like prismatic cohomology) are extremely recent. This tells that human mathematicians are able to go in the right direction. Will AI be able to set the direction too?
